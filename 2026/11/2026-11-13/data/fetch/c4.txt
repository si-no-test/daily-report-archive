DOJ AI Task Force Puts Business Users in Enforcement Spotlight
The Department of Justice ’s new Super Intelligence Task Force isn’t aiming its enforcement efforts at artificial intelligence developers alone. Companies using AI in everyday operations should expect closer scrutiny of how they prevent the technology from facilitating unlawful conduct.
The initiative brings AI more firmly into DOJ’s corporate enforcement infrastructure , according to an analysis by law firm McGuireWoods . Its responsibilities include developing guidance on prosecuting unlawful AI-related conduct and evaluating compliance measures, making internal controls a central concern for businesses deploying the technology.
The DOJ announced the task force Nov. 5, following an Oct. 13 memorandum from Attorney General Todd Blanche . The group will coordinate criminal and civil enforcement involving AI across department components and U.S. attorneys’ offices, absorbing the Artificial Intelligence Task Force announced in January. Super Intelligence is the term used by the executive branch in place of artificial intelligence, according to a Sept. 29 executive order signed by President Donald Trump .
For businesses, the significance extends beyond the advanced systems suggested by its name, according to the analysis. Companies using third-party tools or internally developed AI need to understand their exposure, particularly when the technology affects regulated activities, customer interactions, financial transactions or other consequential decisions.
The task force’s mandate includes organizing AI-related enforcement, resolving overlaps among investigations and cases, and recommending priorities intended to preserve public safety and prevent misuse, the analysis said. It will also coordinate affirmative civil litigation involving AI.
Led by the attorney general or designated officials, the group will draw members from components with criminal, civil, national security, fraud, antitrust and appellate responsibilities. That membership underscores the breadth of potential enforcement interests rather than establishing a single category of AI offense, per the analysis.
The task force will serve as DOJ’s principal contact with the Trump administration’s separate Super Intelligence Force and, where appropriate, other federal agencies. It will also advise on legislative and regulatory proposals intended to promote innovation without burdensome regulation, the analysis said.
A notification process could give senior DOJ officials greater visibility into individual cases. At the task force’s request, department components and U.S. attorneys’ offices are expected to notify it when seeking to open an investigation, before seeking charges and before finalizing a resolution involving AI, according to the analysis. The memorandum calls for a process that does not delay enforcement activity.
The compliance emphasis builds on the DOJ’s 2024 updates to its Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs guidance . These revisions directed prosecutors to consider how companies assess and manage AI risks when reviewing compliance programs. The new task force is charged with developing further guidance on evaluating these measures, the analysis said.
DOJ’s announcement said companies should maintain “vigorous internal controls,” so AI behaves as intended. For corporate users, that points toward scrutiny of oversight and safeguards surrounding deployment, including tools purchased from outside vendors, according to the analysis.
Businesses should map where AI operates throughout their organizations and assess whether these uses could facilitate legal violations, the analysis said. Companies should assign responsibility for AI risks, document controls for high-risk applications and ensure compliance teams have sufficient information and authority to address problems.
Programs should also provide visibility into AI use, establish appropriate human oversight and detect unintended or improper use, according to the analysis. These are operational responsibilities that extend beyond selecting a technology provider or approving a procurement contract.
The task force has been charged with recommending enforcement priorities, rather than announcing a complete list of targeted business practices. Companies should evaluate their governance against the DOJ’s existing AI-related compliance factors before weaknesses become enforcement issues, the analysis said.
